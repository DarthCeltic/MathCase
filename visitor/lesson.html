Move from a concrete counterexample to a theorem, then try a related statement on your own. Each case keeps the plain statement, exact Lean declaration, proof source, and checker receipt as separate parts.

How to read each card: plain statement first, exact declaration next, then the boundary of the result. A checked theorem establishes only the statement written in Lean.

1 · A counterexample repairs a claim

Plain: A composite divisor can divide a square without dividing its root.

(4 : Nat) ∣ 2 ^ 2 ∧ ¬ (4 : Nat) ∣ 2

Shows: the implication “d divides n², therefore d divides n” needs a prime hypothesis.

Does not show: the corresponding statement for primes; that is the next case.

2 · Repair with primality

Plain: If a prime divides a natural number’s square, it divides that number.

∀ (p n : Nat), p.Prime → p ∣ n ^ 2 → p ∣ n

Shows: a known elementary theorem, checked by Lean from the Mathlib prime-divisor lemma.

Does not show: a new result or a general AI theorem-solving capability.

3 · Transfer challenge

Plain: If a prime divides a product squared, prove it divides at least one factor.

∀ (p a b : Nat), p.Prime → p ∣ (a * b) ^ 2 → p ∣ a ∨ p ∣ b

Uses: the prime-divides-square result, followed by the prime-divides-product result.

Does not show: this is a fresh teaching exercise in the workbench, not an evaluated transfer experiment. The teacher explanation is separate.
